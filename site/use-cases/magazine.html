Put the Moon landing on your kitchen table

Three machines that carried us to the Moon and to Mars, at sizes you can walk around. Every figure in this story opens in your room.

By the Perigee desk · 5 min read

On 16 July 1969, a Saturn V lifted Apollo 11 off its pad in Florida. Four days later Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin landed on the Moon in the lunar module Eagle, while Michael Collins circled overhead in Columbia. Most of us know these machines from photographs, and a photograph never tells you how big anything was. So this month, the figures are in 3D: tap one, and it stands in your room.

The real one stood 110.6 metres tall, about the height of a 36-storey building, and its five first-stage engines burned for about two and a half minutes. Thirteen of them flew, between 1967 and 1973. At 1:46 the third stage is at about eye level: the part that pushed Apollo 11 out of Earth orbit and towards the Moon.

In a photo, a rocket is the size of your thumb. In your room, it's taller than you.

Eagle was built to fly only in a vacuum, and it shows: no streamlining anywhere, and in places a skin thinner than a credit card. Its bottom half, the descent stage, served as the launch pad for the top half when the astronauts left. It is still there, in the Sea of Tranquility.

Curiosity is the size of a small car: three metres long and almost 900 kilograms. It landed in Gale crater on Mars in August 2012, lowered on cables from a hovering rocket stage. Its question was whether Mars could ever have supported life, and within its first year it found rock that had formed in water microbes could have lived in.

Put it in your hallway and it fills it. That's the point of this issue: some sizes you only understand when you're standing next to them.

How we made the figures

The three models come from Sketchfab under Creative Commons. Safari opens them in AR by itself; there's nothing to install. If you share a figure from inside AR, you share this article.

How this page uses AR Quick Look

Figures with a caption in AR, and one that stays life-size

Each figure is a link to a .usdz. Its title and subtitle make a caption at the bottom of the AR view, and its Read on button brings you back to the paragraph after the figure. canonicalWebPageURL makes the share button inside AR share the article, not the 3D file, and allowsContentScaling=0 stops the rover from being pinched smaller than it is.
